# In pedestrian-crossing scenes, which combinations of ego speed at hand-off (0.6x–1.4x recorded) and pedestrian step-out time (±2 s) make the VaVAM policy with the linear MPC collide or leave the road? Find the 5 most challenging cases, each on a different scene, confirmed by repeats.

Study `hybrid_optuna_r2`: 40 kept runs on 10 scenes, varying ego_speed_scale, actor_time_shift_s; 1 runs not kept by the gate. Counts are kept runs only.

**Goal (checked by code, not the model):** 5 of 5 challenging settings confirmed, after 4 rounds.

## Answer

The study met its goal. Repeats confirmed five challenging settings, each on a different scene: 18f8dbd6 at 1.4x / +2 s (S15), 0e002edd at 1.4x / −2 s (S9), 1bbe02fc at 0.8x / +1.5 s (S16), 07981e6a at 1.2x / −1 s (S3) and 0d4893f5 at 1.4x / +1.5 s (S7). Every kept run at these settings failed. Still, each has only three or four repeats, so the low end of each 90% range sits just above 0.5 and the true rates are only loosely pinned down. In none of the triaged failures did the ego hit the pedestrian. At every confirmed setting the policy drifted left (or, at S9, turned wide) away from the recorded path, then struck a parked or neighbouring vehicle or left the road. So these cases show the policy failing on lane keeping in pedestrian scenes, not on pedestrian crossings, and the pedestrian's step-out time does not appear to drive them. The S9 case also rests entirely on runs flagged as possible simulator artifacts, because the ego ended more than 3.5 m off the recorded path. Triage still blames the policy for them.

## Open

- No run was made at the nominal setting (1.0x, 0 s) on any confirmed scene. So it is unknown whether these scenes fail anyway because of the left drift, rather than because of the speed–timing combination. A few repeats at 1.0x / 0 s on 18f8dbd6, 0d4893f5, 07981e6a, 1bbe02fc and 0e002edd would settle it.
- No setting produced a pedestrian collision, so the study does not answer which speed/timing combinations make the policy hit the pedestrian. A follow-up could search near the pedestrian's conflict point, and record closest approach to the pedestrian rather than to any actor.
- The 0e002edd case (S9) rests on failures where the ego was far off the recorded path, so rendering could explain them. Video review focused on the rendering, or repeats at a setting that keeps the ego closer to the recorded path, would separate rendering effects from policy behaviour.
- The confirmed cases have only three or four repeats each. A few more repeats per setting would narrow the ranges well above 0.5.
- 048b974e (S1) and 18d28973 (S12) are unresolved: more repeats there, or nearby settings, would show whether they belong in the top cases.
- The comparison with the 10 Hz study pedestrian_ego_speed is not in these results. Running the same five settings at 10 Hz would show whether 2 Hz planning changes them.

## Provenance

Plan: `plan.json` (rationale: Copied from studies/pedestrian_ego_speed/plan.json (10 Hz) with the policy planning every 500 ms, so the method comparison can be repeated at 2 Hz. Scenes: these are the 10 scenes tagged pedestrian_crossing that have a recorded pedestrian key actor. Each scene's pedestrian is set in retime_tracks, so actor_time_shift_s moves only that person. actor_speed_scale stays at 1.0, so the pedestrian walks at the recorded speed. Traffic is replay, which keeps the scenario scripted as in Euro NCAP pedestrian tests. There is no delay, bias or noise.

Two other tagged scenes are left out: 095cf563 and 13a9767e. Neither has a pedestrian key actor, so a person-class retime there might hit no actor or the wrong one.

Budget: 5 rounds of 10 runs gives 50 runs, as the brief asks. The goal is top_k with k=5, high_min=0.5 and distinct scenes. With 90% ranges, each confirmed case needs about 4–5 failures out of 5 repeats, so roughly 25 runs go to confirmation and about 25 to searching the 5×9 grid. That is tight, and with partial results the study may confirm fewer than 5 cases.
