\begin{abstract}
\guide{About 150 words, written last, with the final numbers. Four sentences: (1) the problem: stress testing an end-to-end driving policy in closed loop takes thousands of runs; by hand it is slow, and left unattended it fails silently; (2) what SimGate is: an AI agent that runs the whole campaign, choosing only from fixed menus that code checks and scripts carry out; (3) the evidence: the scale of the unattended campaign, the share of failed launches healed with no person, and that no planted invalid run was kept; (4) the main finding: where failures are rare, letting the agent choose the next tests confirms the hardest cases sooner than rules, random search or Optuna.}
Stress testing a self-driving policy in simulation takes thousands of runs: someone must choose each test, restart the runs that crash and check that each one measured what was asked, and a campaign left unattended fails silently. SimGate hands the whole campaign to an AI agent but never lets it decide what counts: the agent only picks from fixed menus, plain code checks every choice and every run, and scripts carry out what passes. We use it to stress test an end-to-end driving policy on recorded real-world scenes. Over three weeks with no person watching, 99.0\% of 10,920 requested runs ended as usable data; of 467 failed launches, the agent fixed 342 and handed the rest to a person. Runs broken on purpose would have put 294 invalid results in the data; the checks let none through. Where failures are rare, letting the agent choose the next tests found 33 of 35 hardest cases in seven attempts, against at most 13 for rule-based search, random search and Bayesian optimization.
\end{abstract}

Neural-reconstruction simulators now make this possible in closed loop on real recorded scenes, with an end-to-end policy driving from camera images~\cite{ljungbergh2024neuroncap,alpasim}. Left unattended, it can fail silently: a run can finish normally even though the model was misconfigured, a requested setting never took effect, or its results were never saved. Handing the campaign to an AI agent that acts in a fully closed loop does not fix this by itself. An evaluation of an AI-scientist system found many of its experiments failing and numbers in its papers hallucinated~\cite{beel2025sakana}, and data-analysis agents finish without error yet answer the wrong question~\cite{yan2026failloudly}.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\guide{Three short paragraphs. (1) Accelerated and adaptive stress testing (NADE~\cite{feng2021nade}, D2RL~\cite{feng2023d2rl}, adaptive stress testing~\cite{lee2020ast,koren2018ast}, Bayesian optimization~\cite{abeysirigoonawardena2019adversarial,osikowicz2026mfbo}): they supply search algorithms; this paper studies who chooses the next test and how a campaign stays valid. (2) LLMs in scenario generation and testing (LLM-attacker~\cite{mei2025llmattacker}, Teach-to-Crash~\cite{ghazal2026teachtocrash}, EvoDrive~\cite{nie2026evodrive}, PlannerForge~\cite{gao2026plannerforge}, LEADE~\cite{tian2024leade}, and the IV precedents Chat2Scenario~\cite{zhao2024chat2scenario}, EC-Detector~\cite{pokharkar2026ecdetector} and ARISE~\cite{poddubnyy2026arise}): one line each on what they do and how SimGate differs. (3) Agents that act safely (Simplex~\cite{sha2001simplex,seto1998simplex,mehmood2022blackbox}, shielding~\cite{alshiekh2018shield}, runtime rules on agent actions~\cite{wang2026agentspec,shieldagent2025}, self-healing pipelines~\cite{selfhealing2026}), and the evidence that agents running experiments or analyses on their own~\cite{lu2024aiscientist,boiko2023coscientist} produce unreliable results~\cite{beel2025sakana,luo2025pitfalls}, with the auditable runtimes proposed against it~\cite{yan2026failloudly}. Close with one sentence on what sets this paper apart.}

\textit{Stress testing driving policies.} Accelerated and adaptive stress testing search for the rare events where a driving policy fails, by learning where to sample~\cite{feng2021nade,feng2023d2rl}, by sequential decision making~\cite{lee2020ast} or by Bayesian optimization~\cite{abeysirigoonawardena2019adversarial}. They supply search algorithms; this paper asks who chooses the next test and how a campaign that runs without a person stays valid. Neural-reconstruction simulators make such testing possible in closed loop on real recorded scenes~\cite{ljungbergh2024neuroncap,alpasim}.

\textit{LLMs in scenario testing.} LLM-attacker~\cite{mei2025llmattacker}, EC-Detector~\cite{pokharkar2026ecdetector} and ARISE~\cite{poddubnyy2026arise} use an LLM within one step of scenario generation or refinement \verify{check against the papers}. EvoDrive~\cite{nie2026evodrive} lets unattended LLM agents evolve scenario generators, checked by a deterministic validator, for state-based egos in MetaDrive and CARLA, and PlannerForge~\cite{gao2026plannerforge} supports the testing of classical planners through a chat interface. In SimGate the agent runs the whole campaign, search methods are compared at an equal budget under one validity gate, and the policy under test drives from camera images.

\textit{Agents that act safely.} SimGate follows the Simplex architecture~\cite{sha2001simplex}: a simple, verified component decides whether the action of a capable but unverified one is executed.

\section{Framework Architecture and Design}\label{sec:method}
\guide{\textbf{Opening paragraph.} Three or four sentences: SimGate runs a campaign as two nested loops (Fig.~\ref{fig:system}); the agent proposes, code checks and acts. Then what each subsection covers: overview (\ref{sec:overview}), outer loop (\ref{sec:outer}), inner loop (\ref{sec:gate}, \ref{sec:diagnose}), triage and report (\ref{sec:explain}).}

SimGate runs a stress-testing campaign as two nested loops (Fig.~\ref{fig:system}). The outer loop turns a question into a study and chooses which runs to simulate next; the inner loop runs each simulation, checks it and heals it when it fails. Throughout, the agent only proposes: plain code checks every proposal and every run, and scripts carry out what is accepted.

\subsection{Framework Overview}\label{sec:overview}
\guide{Present Fig.~\ref{fig:system} as a whole: a brief comes in, a checked plan sets up a study, the outer loop picks each round of runs, the inner loop runs, checks and heals every run, kept outcomes go back to the goal check, and once the goal is met each failure is explained and a report answers the brief. Explain the colors: orange boxes are the agent, which only proposes; blue boxes are plain code, which checks every proposal and acts on it; grey is a person (who writes the brief, approves the plan, enacts a new rule and takes over a halted run). Define a campaign and a study (scenes, knobs, goal, budget). Then the design rule shared by every orange box: a contract with five parts (when code calls it, what it sees, what it may answer from a fixed menu or schema, who checks the answer, and who acts: a script, never the agent). Leave the concrete simulator and scenarios to Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}.}

A campaign is a set of studies run on one machine without a person. A study answers one question about the policy under test, written as a plain-English brief, for example: when a car cuts in or brakes ahead, which combinations of its timing, its speed and the ego's speed make the policy collide or leave the road? It fixes a set of recorded scenes, the knobs it varies and their legal values, a run budget split into rounds, and a goal.

Every orange box in Fig.~\ref{fig:system} is a contract, run as one fresh model call with no memory of earlier calls. A contract has five parts: when code calls the agent; what it sees, as named inputs and, for some contracts, read-only tools; what it may answer, from a fixed menu or JSON schema; which code checks the answer; and which script acts on it. The agent never acts itself. This follows the Simplex architecture (Sec.~\ref{sec:related}). Seven contracts cover planning a study, choosing the next runs, diagnosing a failed run, auditing a batch, explaining a failure, tagging a scene and writing the report.

\subsection{Outer Loop: Planning and Choosing the Next Runs}\label{sec:outer}
\guide{The top band of Fig.~\ref{fig:system}, left to right. The planner turns the brief into scenes, knobs, rounds and a goal, and code checks the plan before a person approves it. The proposer (rules, a baseline, or the agent) picks each round; every proposed run is checked before it is queued (a candidate scene, legal knob values) and anything else is dropped. Kept outcomes return to the goal check: define a confirmed hardest case precisely (a setting whose failure rate is surely above one half, by a confidence interval over its repeats, at most one per scene), since Sec.~\ref{sec:results} counts them. List the search methods that can act as the proposer (rules, random search with repeats, Optuna~\cite{akiba2019optuna} with repeats, and the agent alone or combined with either), all under the same gate and budget.}

\textit{Planning.} The planner turns the brief into scenes, knob ranges, rounds and a goal; code checks that every scene and knob exists and that the budget stays under a cap, and a person approves the plan. Each round a proposer picks ten runs, and a run with a scene outside the plan or an illegal knob value is dropped.

\textit{The goal.} Every study asks for the $k=5$ hardest cases, at most one per scene. A setting is a \emph{confirmed hardest case} once the 90\% Wilson interval of its failure rate over its repeats lies entirely above one half; three failures in three runs is the fewest that qualifies. A study stops when the goal is met or the budget is spent.

\textit{Proposers.} Only the proposer changes between methods: \emph{rules}, which start at the middle of each knob's range, repeat the setting closest to failing and step each knob one notch from it; \emph{random search} and \emph{Optuna}~\cite{akiba2019optuna}, a widely used Bayesian optimization library, both \emph{with repeats}, meaning up to half of each round reruns near failures, since a search that never repeats a setting cannot confirm one; the \emph{agent}; and the agent restricted to the candidates of the rules or of Optuna. Each round the agent sees every kept run, a per-setting summary with failure-rate intervals and the goal's status. Its second version, evaluated in Sec.~\ref{sec:discovery}, also sees triage's explanation of each failure (Sec.~\ref{sec:explain}), facts about each scene and a note it wrote itself the round before; its instructions were revised once, on the 10\,Hz studies, and frozen before the two main studies ran.

\subsection{Inner Loop: Running and Checking Each Run}\label{sec:gate}
\guide{The upper row of the bottom band of Fig.~\ref{fig:system}. Each run passes checks before launch, runs in closed loop, and passes checks after it, in a fixed order; it is kept only if the requested scenario provably happened (read back from its logs) and its motion is physically plausible. Stress that code, not the model, decides that a run failed and names the reason. Define the outcome a kept run carries: a failure counts only if the policy is at fault after it takes over. Then the batch audit and how new checks are learned: the auditor flags runs that are wrong in the same way and proposes rules; code admits a rule only if it catches the flagged runs and fires on no clean run; a person enacts it; from then on it is checked on every run \emph{(Fig.~\ref{fig:system} does not show the auditor yet; add it when the figure is redrawn)}.}

Before launch, a preflight check rejects a configuration the simulator would not run as asked, and a machine check confirms free GPU memory and disk, no leftover containers and a free container network. After the run, postflight checks run in a fixed order: the exit code; a readable metrics file; that every requested setting landed in the configuration the simulator resolved; that each varied knob took effect, read back from the run's own log; that the motion is physically plausible (acceleration, jerk, yaw rate, and reported against implied speed, within what a road car can do); and every rule admitted by the batch audit. Code, not the model, decides that a run failed and names the reason. In a kept run, a failure counts only if it is the policy's: the ego leaves the road, or a front or side collision begins after the hand-off to the policy.

After each batch, the auditor compares its runs with a reference run of each scene, flags runs that are wrong in the same way and proposes rules to catch them. Code admits a rule only if it fires on every flagged run and on no clean run; a person enacts it, and from then on it is checked on every run with no model call. One such rule, that the requested controller is the one that ran, catches a silent controller fault.

When a run fails, code calls the diagnose contract once. The agent sees the failure's reason, the configuration, the attempt count, up to 15 error lines and a machine snapshot, and may use read-only tools on the run's files and the machine; the tools are read-only by permission, not by instruction. It answers one of five skills from a fixed schema: RE-RUN queues the run again; CONFIGURE changes how it executes (context length, scene file, where the traffic model runs), never what it measures; RESTART\_CLEANUP takes down its containers and reruns it; CLEANUP\_ENV clears leftovers from the whole machine, at most once per run; HALT stops it for a person and is always accepted. Every skill but HALT needs an attempt left, with at most three launches per run.

Because the menu is finite and the state small, every answer the agent could give was tried against every failure state: the real validator and recovery script over 49 states and 3,822 transitions, checking that no failed run is kept, no run launches more than three times, no recovery loops and no kept run measures a setting other than the one requested. The first pass found one violation, a CONFIGURE that could silently undo the planner delay under test; that key was removed, which is why a recovery may change how a run executes but never what it measures.


\subsection{Closing a Study: Triage and Report}\label{sec:explain}
\guide{The right end of the top band of Fig.~\ref{fig:system}. Triage: for each failed run the agent sees frames before the failure and the metrics, and answers a cause from a fixed list and whether the policy was at fault; counts over these answers are computed by code. Report: the agent answers the brief by citing setting ids; code writes every number, and a report with a hand-written count is refused.}

For each failed run, the triage contract sees frames before and at the failure with the speed and the gap to the nearest road user, and answers a cause from a fixed list (no braking for the car ahead, steered into a road user, left the road, a road user drove into the ego, rendering fault, other) and whether the policy was at fault. Code computes every count over these answers, and an explanation never decides which runs are kept. The report answers the brief by citing setting identifiers; code writes every number, and a report with a hand-written count is refused.

\subsection{Simulation Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\guide{Two parts. \textbf{Simulation:} the simulator and policy (AlpaSim~\cite{alpasim}, VaVAM~\cite{bartoccioni2025vavam}, linear MPC tracking, replayed traffic; planning at 2 Hz and 10 Hz), the knobs, the test scenarios (studies mapped to Euro NCAP tests~\cite{euroncap2023vru,euroncap2023c2c}), repeats and run budgets per method, the metrics (confirmed hardest cases, runs to reach the goal, model time) and the statistical tests. \textbf{Computing environment:} one workstation (GPU model and memory, CPU, RAM, disk, operating system) running two simulations at once on its single GPU; the simulator in containers, with the added retiming and seeding hooks off by default; the agent's model and how it is called (one fresh headless call per decision, read-only tools, time per call); run time (simulated seconds and wall-clock per run at each planning rate, total GPU hours). \emph{Confirm the exact hardware and software versions for the whole campaign; a short table is a common way to report them.}}

\textit{Simulation.} Runs use AlpaSim~\cite{alpasim}, which replays recorded drives as neural reconstructions and closes the loop with the policy under test, VaVAM~\cite{bartoccioni2025vavam}, using the 139k-step checkpoint AlpaSim provides. A linear MPC tracks its plan, and other road users replay their recorded tracks. The ego replays its own recording for the first 4.5\,s, then the policy takes over. Planning runs at VaVAM's own 2\,Hz rhythm or at 10\,Hz with the camera at 2\,Hz. The knobs are a recorded actor's time shift ($-2$ to $+2$\,s) and speed scale ($0.5\times$ to $2\times$), applied by a retiming hook added to AlpaSim's runtime; the ego's speed at the hand-off ($0.6\times$ to $1.4\times$ its recorded speed); and a sideways offset of the plan ($-0.5$ to $+0.5$\,m).

\textit{Studies.} Two main studies were run after the agent's instructions were frozen. In \emph{car cut-in and braking}, a car cuts in or brakes ahead of the ego on 10 scenes that pass as recorded; three knobs give 2,700 settings, the budget is 60 runs at 2\,Hz, and failures are rare. In \emph{lane keeping}, the plan is offset sideways on 12 straight scenes while the ego's speed varies; the budget is 50 runs at 10\,Hz. Three earlier studies at 10\,Hz (a pedestrian crossing, a car cutting in, a car ahead braking) map to the Euro NCAP AEB VRU and car-to-car tests~\cite{euroncap2023vru,euroncap2023c2c}. Each method runs each main study from scratch \verify{7} times and each earlier study 3 times.

\textit{Metrics.} Per attempt: hardest cases confirmed at the budget and runs to reach the goal. The planned comparisons, the agent against random search, Optuna and the rules on the two main studies, use an exact two-sided permutation test, Holm-corrected over the three; every other comparison is descriptive.

\textit{Computing environment.} Everything ran on one workstation (NVIDIA RTX 4500 Ada, 24\,GB; Intel Core Ultra 9 285; 62\,GB RAM; Ubuntu 22.04), two simulations at once on its single GPU, with AlpaSim \verify{version} in containers and the added retiming and seeding hooks off by default. The agent is Claude Opus~5.5, called once per decision through the Claude Code command line. A run takes about 3\,min with launch and checks (rollouts of 47\,s at 2\,Hz and 84\,s at 10\,Hz); the campaign used \verify{N} GPU hours.

\subsection{Validity of Kept Runs}\label{sec:validity}
\guide{Test whether every kept run is real data, in both directions: bad runs must be caught and good runs kept. (1) Planted faults: in three campaigns, runs were broken on purpose in ways that still end normally (deleted or corrupted results, a requested setting silently dropped, the recorded human driving the whole run, no controller in the loop). Count the invalid runs kept with no gate, with the per-run gate, and with the batch audit added, and give an upper bound on the share that could still slip through. Say which faults only the batch audit catches and why (they look the same in every run of a batch). (2) False alarms: the physics check on clean runs (none should be thrown away) and on runs with silent motion faults (all should be caught). (3) Close by noting that every later result uses kept runs only. \emph{Add a small table, generated from the data: invalid runs kept per planted-fault campaign with no gate, the per-run gate, and gate + audit. No figure is needed.}}

Four campaigns (C1--C4) plant faults on purpose. Each plans 50 runs, five of each of eight fault kinds and ten clean runs, once per recovery handler of Sec.~\ref{sec:healing}. Five kinds are silent: the run ends normally, but its results were deleted or corrupted, a requested setting was dropped, the recorded human drove the whole run, or no controller was in the loop. C1 ran with the physics bounds off and C2--C4 with them on; in C3 and C4 the silent faults repeat on every retry, as a configuration bug would, and C4 repeats C3 on six scenes no other campaign used.

Table~\ref{tab:validity} counts the invalid runs kept. Keeping every run that ends normally would have kept 294. \subsection{Self-Healing in Unattended Operation}\label{sec:healing}
\guide{Test whether the agent keeps a long campaign running without a person, using every failed launch of the whole campaign. (1) Scale and outcome: runs asked for, runs kept on the first try, launches that failed and went to the agent, and how many of those were healed or stopped for a person (Fig.~\ref{fig:healing}). (2) What the agent chose: for each kind of failure, the skill it picked and how the run ended; every answer passed the validator, and every retry ran exactly the settings asked for. (3) Honest accounting: most heals are out-of-memory crashes answered by a plain rerun, which a retry script would also do; the agent's value is in the rest, such as halting runs that failed the physics check instead of rerunning them and clearing a machine that was not ready. From 6 to 28 October the campaign asked for 10,920 runs and made 11,252 launches, unattended (Fig.~\ref{fig:healing}); 10,492 runs were kept on the first try. The 467 launches that failed went to the agent, which healed 342, whose reruns were kept, and stopped 125 for a person; in all, 10,810 runs (99.0\%) ended as kept data. Every answer passed the validator, every retry ran exactly the settings asked for (467 of 467), and the median decision took 8.3\,s. When the disk filled on 22 October, the machine check failed every launch and the agent stopped each run for a person, as designed; those arms were rerun and are left out of these counts.

By kind of failure, 312 were GPU out-of-memory crashes, answered with RE-RUN 307 times; 104 were runs that failed the physics check, all halted, since a rerun reproduces the fault; 25 were launches onto a GPU still held by the previous run, answered with CLEANUP\_ENV 24 times; and 26 were other crashes. Most heals are the plain rerun a retry script would also make.

The planted faults isolate what the agent adds (Table~\ref{tab:recovery}): in C3 and C4 the same faults went to a fixed retry script, to the agent without tools and to the agent with read-only tools. The script kept all 35 runs that can end as valid data but spent 95 and 102 launches, retrying the 15 faults that repeat on every retry until the limit. The agent without tools spent the fewest launches but kept only 25 and 28, halting runs whose results were lost after a normal exit, which a rerun fixes. With read-only tools the agent kept all 35 in 72 launches in both campaigns.

\subsection{Corner-Case Discovery Across Search Methods}\label{sec:discovery}
\guide{Test whether letting the agent choose the next tests finds and confirms the hardest cases sooner, at an equal budget under the same gate. (1) The comparison: each method replaces only the proposer; studies, budgets, rounds and goal stay the same; each method runs several repeats. Report confirmed hardest cases at the budget and runs to reach the goal (Fig.~\ref{fig:discovery}, Table~\ref{tab:methods}). (2) Main result first: the three-knob study, where failures are rare, with five repeats and comparisons planned before the extra repeats were run; then lane keeping, a study the second AI version was not built on; then the car scenes and the pedestrian study, where failures are common. (3) The pattern: the agent's lead grows as failures get rarer, and where failures are common it has no edge (say so); Optuna alone does no better than random search with repeats; the agent restricted to the rules' candidates does well only where those candidates are good. Explain it by where each method looked: the rules start in the middle of each knob's range and step locally, random search and Optuna spread runs thin, the agent uses the physics stated in the brief and repeats what fails. (4) Counting decides the ranking: under a naive collision count (contacts begun before the hand-off, rear-ends by replayed traffic) the ranking changes; show it briefly. \emph{Fig.~\ref{fig:discovery} and Table~\ref{tab:methods} are placeholders with the three 10 Hz studies only; add the three-knob study and lane keeping, ordered from rare to common failures. Compute every test statistic with code.}}

Each method replaces only the proposer of Fig.~\ref{fig:system}: the study, its budget, the rounds of ten runs, the rerun rule and the gate stay the same, and every method runs each main study seven times from scratch. Table~\ref{tab:methods} gives every method on every study.

Car cut-in and braking is the hardest search: of the 814 settings any method tried, only 66 fail reliably. In seven attempts each, the agent confirmed 33 of a possible 35 hardest cases (all five in five attempts, four in the other two), Optuna with repeats 13, random search with repeats 10 and the rules none (Fig.~\ref{fig:discovery}, left). The separation is complete: every attempt of the agent beats every attempt of each baseline, an exact two-sided $p=0.0006$ for each planned comparison, the smallest possible with seven attempts each (0.0017 after Holm's correction). On lane keeping every method but the rules reaches the goal, so the runs it takes decide: the agent needed 17--21 runs (median 19), random search 23--40 (30) and Optuna 25--40 (30), and the rules never got there within 50 (Fig.~\ref{fig:discovery}, right), again with complete separation ($p=0.0006$, Holm 0.0017). The other two AI proposers are close to the agent on lane keeping (first prompt 19--22 runs, Optuna + AI 18--21), but not on the rarer failures of car cut-in and braking (22 and 16 of 35 confirmed).

Where failures are common, the agent has no edge: on the 10\,Hz car scenes the AI choosing among the rules' candidates led (17 of 30), and on the pedestrian study every method reached the goal (Table~\ref{tab:methods}).

Where each method looked explains the ranking. On car cut-in and braking, 28\% of the agent's runs landed on a setting that fails reliably, against 12\% for Optuna, 7\% for random search and none for the rules, and the agent reached all 7 scenes that have one (random search and Optuna 5, the rules 0). The rules step one notch at a time from the recorded setting, where these scenes pass; random search and Optuna spread their runs over the whole grid; the agent uses the physics stated in the brief, such as a slower car ahead or a faster ego, and repeats what fails.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
\guide{\textbf{Paragraph 1: summary.} Restate the two main claims in a few sentences: an agent can run stress testing of a driving policy end to end, unattended, when each action is a checked choice from a fixed menu; and letting it choose the next tests pays off most where failures are rare. Say where the code and data are released.}

When each action of the agent is a checked choice from a fixed menu, it can run stress testing of an end-to-end driving policy for weeks without a person, and letting it choose the next tests pays off most where failures are rare. The code, briefs, failure ledger and case cards are released at \texttt{github.com/willv678/simgate}.


\guide{\textbf{Paragraph 2: limitations and future work.} Pair each limitation with the direction that addresses it: one policy under test (test more end-to-end policies); replayed traffic does not react (use reactive traffic); few scenes for some maneuvers (add more scenario types); few repeats per method (run more repeats, and try other models as the agent). Also state the limitations with no direction yet: rendering is less certain far from the recording, and the human check of the scoring is still pending. End with the confirmed hardest cases reused as regression tests.}

SimGate was tested on one policy with replayed traffic that does not react to the ego and on recorded scenes whose weather, lighting and road geometry it cannot change; more policies, reactive traffic and simulators that generate new conditions are the next steps. Each method ran \verify{7} attempts per main study, with one model as the agent. Rendering is less certain far from the recorded path, where some failures happen (they are flagged). Every confirmed hardest case is exported as an OpenSCENARIO~1.4 scenario, so it can be reused as a regression test.
