\begin{abstract}
\guide{About 150 words, written last, with the final numbers. Four sentences: (1) the problem: stress testing an end-to-end driving policy in closed loop takes thousands of runs; by hand it is slow, and left unattended it fails silently; (2) what SimGate is: an AI agent that runs the whole campaign, choosing only from fixed menus that code checks and scripts carry out; (3) the evidence: the scale of the unattended campaign, the share of failed launches healed with no person, and that no planted invalid run was kept; (4) the main finding: where failures are rare, letting the agent choose the next tests confirms the hardest cases sooner than rules, random search or Optuna.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
\guide{\textbf{Paragraph 1: background and gap.} Motivate accelerated stress testing of end-to-end driving policies~\cite{feng2021nade,feng2023d2rl}, now possible in closed loop on real recorded scenes with neural-reconstruction simulators~\cite{ljungbergh2024neuroncap,zhou2024hugsim,alpasim}. State the gap: a campaign is thousands of runs that someone must choose, relaunch when they crash, and verify; left unattended it fails silently (use concrete early examples: a misconfigured model, a delay that never applied, missing results), and simulation results can themselves be flaky or nondeterministic ~\cite{osikowicz2025flaky,amini2024flaky,chance2022determinism}. Then the second half of the gap: handing the campaign to an AI agent that acts in a fully closed loop does not fix this by itself. An evaluation of an AI-scientist system found many experiments failing and hallucinated numbers in its papers~\cite{beel2025sakana}; such systems hide pitfalls that only full trace logs reveal~\cite{luo2025pitfalls}; coding agents make checks pass by deleting failing tests~\cite{zhong2025impossiblebench}; data-analysis agents finish without error yet answer the wrong question~\cite{yan2026failloudly}. Checking has not kept pace with what agents produce~\cite{mo2026trustworthy}.}

\guide{\textbf{Paragraph 2: approach and core contributions.} Open with the approach: hand the whole campaign to an AI agent but never let it decide what counts: it only proposes from fixed menus, code checks every proposal and every run, and every failure is kept with its evidence. Then list the contributions, each naming where it is described and where it is validated. (1) An agent that runs stress testing of an end-to-end driving policy end to end and unattended, in a neural-reconstruction simulator (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}; Sec.~\ref{sec:healing}). (2) An action design that makes it trustworthy: every job is a contract with a fixed menu, checked by code and carried out by scripts; the recovery menu is checked exhaustively, and every kept run is verified against its request (Sec.~\ref{sec:overview}, \ref{sec:gate}, \ref{sec:diagnose}; Sec.~\ref{sec:validity}). (3) Evidence that letting the agent choose the next tests confirms the hardest cases sooner where failures are rare, at an equal budget against rules, random search and Optuna (Sec.~\ref{sec:outer}; Sec.~\ref{sec:discovery}). (4) Results a person can check: every failure the agent handled with its evidence, a case card for every confirmed hardest case, and the agent's failure explanations checked against rules and people (Sec.~\ref{sec:healing}, \ref{sec:triage}, \ref{sec:cases}). Do not claim to be the ``first''.}

\guide{\textbf{Paragraph 3: organization of the paper.} One sentence per section. Sec.~\ref{sec:related} reviews accelerated stress testing, LLMs in scenario generation and testing, and agents that act safely. Sec.~\ref{sec:method} presents the framework around Fig.~\ref{fig:system}: its overview, the outer loop that plans a study and chooses the next runs, the inner loop that checks each run and heals failed ones, and how a study closes with triage and a report. Sec.~\ref{sec:results} describes the simulation setup and validates the framework: the validity of kept runs, self-healing in unattended operation, corner-case discovery across search methods, the reliability of failure triage, and the failures found in the policy under test. Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with limitations and future work.}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\guide{Three short paragraphs. (1) Accelerated and adaptive stress testing (NADE~\cite{feng2021nade}, D2RL~\cite{feng2023d2rl}, adaptive stress testing~\cite{lee2020ast,koren2018ast}, Bayesian optimization~\cite{abeysirigoonawardena2019adversarial,osikowicz2026mfbo}): they supply search algorithms; this paper studies who chooses the next test and how a campaign stays valid. (2) LLMs in scenario generation and testing (LLM-attacker~\cite{mei2025llmattacker}, Teach-to-Crash~\cite{ghazal2026teachtocrash}, EvoDrive~\cite{nie2026evodrive}, PlannerForge~\cite{gao2026plannerforge}, LEADE~\cite{tian2024leade}, and the IV precedents Chat2Scenario~\cite{zhao2024chat2scenario}, EC-Detector~\cite{pokharkar2026ecdetector} and ARISE~\cite{poddubnyy2026arise}): one line each on what they do and how SimGate differs. (3) Agents that act safely (Simplex~\cite{sha2001simplex,seto1998simplex,mehmood2022blackbox}, shielding~\cite{alshiekh2018shield}, runtime rules on agent actions~\cite{wang2026agentspec,shieldagent2025}, self-healing pipelines~\cite{selfhealing2026}), and the evidence that agents running experiments or analyses on their own~\cite{lu2024aiscientist,boiko2023coscientist} produce unreliable results~\cite{beel2025sakana,luo2025pitfalls}, with the auditable runtimes proposed against it~\cite{yan2026failloudly}. Close with one sentence on what sets this paper apart.}

\textit{Accelerated and adaptive stress testing.} Naturalistic and adversarial environments learn where a driving policy is likely to fail and sample there, so that rare events appear far more often than on the road~\cite{feng2021nade,feng2023d2rl}. Adaptive stress testing casts the search for failures as a sequential decision problem~\cite{lee2020ast,koren2018ast}, and Bayesian optimization searches a scenario's parameters for them~\cite{abeysirigoonawardena2019adversarial,osikowicz2026mfbo}. These works supply search algorithms. This paper studies a different question: who chooses the next test, and how a campaign that runs without a person stays valid. Closed-loop testing of end-to-end policies on real recorded scenes became possible with neural-reconstruction simulators~\cite{ljungbergh2024neuroncap,zhou2024hugsim,alpasim}, on which SimGate builds.

\textit{LLMs in scenario generation and testing.} LLM-attacker~\cite{mei2025llmattacker} and Teach-to-Crash~\cite{ghazal2026teachtocrash} place an LLM inside one step of scenario generation and score the result by collision rate. LEADE~\cite{tian2024leade} builds test scenarios from driving videos offline, and Chat2Scenario~\cite{zhao2024chat2scenario} extracts them from datasets. EC-Detector~\cite{pokharkar2026ecdetector} and ARISE~\cite{poddubnyy2026arise} use AI to find edge cases and to refine scenarios iteratively \verify{check one line each against the papers}. EvoDrive~\cite{nie2026evodrive} lets unattended LLM agents evolve adversarial scenario generators, checks their proposals with a deterministic validator and compares them with non-LLM search, in MetaDrive and CARLA with state-based egos. PlannerForge~\cite{gao2026plannerforge} covers the testing lifecycle through a chat interface, for classical planners in CommonRoad. SimGate differs in what the agent operates and how it is checked: the agent runs the whole campaign (it plans, runs, heals, checks, explains and reports), search strategies are compared at an equal budget under one validity gate, and the policy under test maps camera images to trajectories in neural reconstructions of real drives.

\textit{Agents that act safely.} SimGate follows the Simplex architecture~\cite{sha2001simplex,seto1998simplex}, in which a simple, verified component decides whether the action of a capable but unverified one is executed, including its black-box form~\cite{mehmood2022blackbox}. It is related to shielding in reinforcement learning~\cite{alshiekh2018shield}, to deterministic admissibility checks on LLM advice~\cite{misra2026admitbench,vyas2026detection}, to runtime rules on agent actions~\cite{wang2026agentspec,shieldagent2025}, and to self-healing data pipelines~\cite{selfhealing2026}. Agents that run experiments or analyses on their own~\cite{lu2024aiscientist,boiko2023coscientist} have been found to report failed experiments and invented numbers~\cite{beel2025sakana,luo2025pitfalls}, and auditable runtimes have been proposed in response~\cite{yan2026failloudly}. To our knowledge, no prior work runs an unattended stress-testing campaign of an end-to-end driving policy in which every action of the agent is checked by code and every kept run is verified against its request.

\section{Framework Architecture and Design}\label{sec:method}
\guide{\textbf{Opening paragraph.} Three or four sentences: SimGate runs a campaign as two nested loops (Fig.~\ref{fig:system}); the agent proposes, code checks and acts. Then what each subsection covers: overview (\ref{sec:overview}), outer loop (\ref{sec:outer}), inner loop (\ref{sec:gate}, \ref{sec:diagnose}), triage and report (\ref{sec:explain}).}

SimGate runs a stress-testing campaign as two nested loops (Fig.~\ref{fig:system}). The outer loop turns a question into a study and chooses which runs to simulate next; the inner loop runs each simulation, checks it, and heals it when it fails. Throughout, an AI agent only proposes: plain code checks every proposal and every run, and scripts carry out what is accepted. Sec.~\ref{sec:overview} gives the overview and the design rule shared by every step the agent takes, Sec.~\ref{sec:outer} the outer loop, Sec.~\ref{sec:gate} and~\ref{sec:diagnose} the inner loop, and Sec.~\ref{sec:explain} how a study closes.

\subsection{Framework Overview}\label{sec:overview}
\guide{Present Fig.~\ref{fig:system} as a whole: a brief comes in, a checked plan sets up a study, the outer loop picks each round of runs, the inner loop runs, checks and heals every run, kept outcomes go back to the goal check, and once the goal is met each failure is explained and a report answers the brief. Explain the colors: orange boxes are the agent, which only proposes; blue boxes are plain code, which checks every proposal and acts on it; grey is a person (who writes the brief, approves the plan, enacts a new rule and takes over a halted run). Define a campaign and a study (scenes, knobs, goal, budget). Then the design rule shared by every orange box: a contract with five parts (when code calls it, what it sees, what it may answer from a fixed menu or schema, who checks the answer, and who acts: a script, never the agent). Leave the concrete simulator and scenarios to Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}.}

A campaign is a set of studies run on one machine without a person. A study answers one question about the policy under test, written in plain English as a brief, for example: when a car cuts in or brakes ahead of the ego, which combinations of its timing, its speed and the ego's speed at hand-off make the policy collide or leave the road? A study fixes a set of recorded scenes, the knobs it varies and their legal values, a run budget split into rounds, and a goal that code checks after every round. Fig.~\ref{fig:system} follows a study through: the brief becomes a checked plan, the outer loop picks each round of runs, the inner loop runs, checks and heals every run, kept outcomes return to the goal check, and once the goal is met or the budget is spent each failure is explained and a report answers the brief. Orange boxes are the agent, which only proposes; blue boxes are plain code, which checks every proposal and acts on it; grey is a person, who writes the brief, approves the plan, enacts a new rule and takes over a halted run.

Every orange box follows the same contract, written as one markdown file and run as one fresh model call with no memory of earlier calls. A contract has five parts: when code calls the agent; what the agent sees, as named input fields and, for some contracts, read-only tools; what it may answer, a fixed menu or a JSON schema, with free text only where a person reads it; which code checks the answer before anything happens; and which script acts on an accepted answer. The agent never acts itself. This is the structure of the Simplex architecture~\cite{sha2001simplex}: a capable but untrusted component proposes, and a simple, tested component decides what is executed. Seven contracts cover planning a study, choosing the next runs, diagnosing a failed run, auditing a batch, explaining a failure, tagging a scene and writing the report. They are also packaged as Claude Agent Skills, so an agent working in the repository can pick the right one by itself.

\subsection{Outer Loop: Planning and Choosing the Next Runs}\label{sec:outer}
\guide{The top band of Fig.~\ref{fig:system}, left to right. The planner turns the brief into scenes, knobs, rounds and a goal, and code checks the plan before a person approves it. The proposer (rules, a baseline, or the agent) picks each round; every proposed run is checked before it is queued (a candidate scene, legal knob values) and anything else is dropped. Kept outcomes return to the goal check: define a confirmed hardest case precisely (a setting whose failure rate is surely above one half, by a confidence interval over its repeats, at most one per scene), since Sec.~\ref{sec:results} counts them. List the search methods that can act as the proposer (rules, random search with repeats, Optuna~\cite{akiba2019optuna} with repeats, and the agent alone or combined with either), all under the same gate and budget.}

\textit{Planning.} The planner contract turns the brief into a plan: the scenes, the knobs and their ranges, the number of rounds and of runs per round, and the goal. Code checks the plan before a person approves it: every knob and scene must exist, the goal must be well formed, and the budget must stay under a cap.

\textit{Choosing the next runs.} Each round, a proposer picks the next ten runs. Every proposed run is checked before it is queued: its scene must be one of the plan's and every knob value must be legal; anything else is dropped and logged.

\textit{The goal.} Every study in this paper asks for the $k=5$ hardest cases, at most one per scene, so that a method cannot find one bad scene and count five variations of it. A setting is a \emph{confirmed hardest case} once the 90\% Wilson interval of its failure rate over its repeats lies entirely above one half; three failures in three runs is the fewest that qualifies. Settings are ranked by the lower end of that interval, then by mean criticality, a score that is 1 for a failure and approaches 1 for near misses. A study stops when the goal is met or the budget is spent.

\textit{Proposers.} Any of the following can act as the proposer, and nothing else changes between them: \emph{rules}, which start each scene with every knob at the middle of its range, repeat the setting that came closest to failing, and step each knob one notch either way from it; \emph{random search with repeats}; \emph{Optuna}~\cite{akiba2019optuna} with its TPE sampler maximizing criticality, \emph{with repeats}; the \emph{agent}, choosing freely; and the agent restricted to the candidates of the rules or of Optuna. ``With repeats'' means that up to half of each round confirms the rules' near failures, since a search that never repeats a setting cannot confirm one. Each round the agent sees every kept run so far, a per-setting summary with failure-rate intervals, and the goal's status. Its second version, the one evaluated in Sec.~\ref{sec:discovery}, also sees each failure's explanation from triage (Sec.~\ref{sec:explain}), facts about each scene's recording, and a note it wrote to itself in the previous round; its instructions were revised once, on the three 10\,Hz studies, and frozen before the two main studies ran.

\subsection{Inner Loop: Running and Checking Each Run}\label{sec:gate}
\guide{The upper row of the bottom band of Fig.~\ref{fig:system}. Each run passes checks before launch, runs in closed loop, and passes checks after it, in a fixed order; it is kept only if the requested scenario provably happened (read back from its logs) and its motion is physically plausible. Stress that code, not the model, decides that a run failed and names the reason. Define the outcome a kept run carries: a failure counts only if the policy is at fault after it takes over. Then the batch audit and how new checks are learned: the auditor flags runs that are wrong in the same way and proposes rules; code admits a rule only if it catches the flagged runs and fires on no clean run; a person enacts it; from then on it is checked on every run \emph{(Fig.~\ref{fig:system} does not show the auditor yet; add it when the figure is redrawn)}.}

Each run is a queue entry that moves through fixed states (ready, running, complete, failed, done), and one script owns each state. Before launch, a preflight check rejects a configuration the simulator would not run as asked, and a machine check confirms the machine can take the run: enough free GPU memory, at least 20\,GB of free disk, no containers left over from an ended run, a free container network, and fewer runs in flight than the GPU holds. The run then executes in closed loop. After it, postflight checks run in a fixed order: the launcher's exit code; a readable metrics file; that every requested setting landed, by comparing the request with the configuration the simulator resolved; that each varied knob took effect, read back from the run's own log (the runtime's retiming record for actor timing and speed, the measured speed against the request for ego speed); that the motion is physically plausible, from acceleration, jerk and yaw rate and the agreement of reported and implied speed, against bounds a road car can reach; and every rule admitted by the batch audit. A run that passes all of them is kept; a run that fails one is marked failed with the reason. Code, not the model, decides both.

A kept run carries its outcome. A failure counts only if it is the policy's: the ego leaves the road, or a front or side collision begins before any rear contact, counted from the policy's first step after the hand-off. Contacts with a replayed actor that the retiming has frozen in place are not counted.

After a batch, the auditor contract reviews its runs, their configurations and metrics, and a reference run of each scene; it flags runs that are wrong in the same way and proposes rules that would catch them. Code admits a rule only if it fires on every flagged run and on no clean run; a person then enacts it, and from then on it is checked on every run with no model call. One admitted rule, that the controller requested is the one that ran, now catches a silent controller fault on every run.

\subsection{Inner Loop: Healing Failed Runs}\label{sec:diagnose}
\guide{The lower row of the bottom band of Fig.~\ref{fig:system}, zoomed in twice. Fig.~\ref{fig:contract} zooms into that row: the five steps around the investigator, of which only the third is the agent. Fig.~\ref{fig:io} zooms further into steps 2 and 3: what the agent is given and what it may return. Walk through them: the diagnose contract is called only on a failed run and sees the failure status, the config, the attempt count, error lines and a machine snapshot, with read-only tools. HALT stops the run for a person; it is always accepted, and after a machine failure it stops the whole batch. Every skill but HALT needs an attempt left. Code restricts the choice before launch and through the attempt budget, while after launch every skill is allowed and the choice is the agent's (for example, halting every physically wrong run). Note that the tools are read-only by permission, not by instruction. End with the exhaustive check of every answer the agent could give. \emph{Redraw Fig.~\ref{fig:contract} in the paper's style: title it by its role, the inner loop's investigator, and show only the five steps; campaign counts belong in Sec.~\ref{sec:healing}, not in this figure.}}

Fig.~\ref{fig:contract} zooms into the lower row of the bottom band, and Fig.~\ref{fig:io} into its second and third steps. When a run fails, code calls the diagnose contract once, in a fresh call. The agent sees the failure's status and reason, the run's configuration, its attempt count and limit, up to 15 error lines, and a snapshot of the machine taken when the failure was detected; it may use read-only tools on the run's folder and log and on the machine (containers, networks, GPU memory, disk). The tools are read-only by permission, not by instruction. It answers one of five skills and its parameters, from a fixed schema with no free text:
\begin{itemize}
\item RE-RUN queues the same run again.
\item CONFIGURE changes how the run executes (the context length, the scene file, or where the traffic model runs), never what it measures.
\item RESTART\_CLEANUP takes down this run's containers, then reruns it.
\item CLEANUP\_ENV clears leftovers from the whole machine (containers of ended runs and unused networks); it is the only fix allowed when the machine was not ready, and only once per run.
\item HALT stops the run for a person. It is always accepted, and after a machine failure it stops the whole batch.
\end{itemize}
Every skill but HALT needs an attempt left, with at most three launches per run. Before launch, code restricts the choice to the skills that can address the failure; after launch every skill is allowed and the choice is the agent's, for example halting a run whose motion failed the physics check rather than rerunning it, since a rerun of the same setting reproduces the fault. A recovery queues a new attempt in a new folder and never touches the failed run's files.

Because the menu is finite and the state is small, every answer the agent could give can be tried against every failure state. An exhaustive check runs the real validator and recovery script over all of them, 49 states and 3,822 transitions: no failed run is kept, no rejected configuration launches, no run launches more than three times, no recovery loops back on itself, CLEANUP\_ENV runs at most once per run, and no kept run measures a setting other than the one requested. Its first pass found one violation: a CONFIGURE of the planner delay could silently undo the delay under test and still yield a kept run. That key was removed from the configurable set, which is why a recovery may change how a run executes but never what it measures.

\subsection{Closing a Study: Triage and Report}\label{sec:explain}
\guide{The right end of the top band of Fig.~\ref{fig:system}. Triage: for each failed run the agent sees frames before the failure and the metrics, and answers a cause from a fixed list and whether the policy was at fault; counts over these answers are computed by code. Report: the agent answers the brief by citing setting ids; code writes every number, and a report with a hand-written count is refused.}

When a run fails, the triage contract explains it. It sees frames from before the failure and at it, with the speed and the gap to the nearest road user over the last seconds, and answers a cause from a fixed list (no braking for the lead vehicle, steered into a road user, left the road, a road user drove into the ego, rendering fault, other), whether the policy was at fault, and one paragraph for a person. Code computes every count over these answers, and an explanation never decides which runs are kept or counted. The agent's second version reads these explanations every round when it chooses the next runs (Sec.~\ref{sec:outer}). When the study ends, the report contract answers the brief by citing setting identifiers; code writes every number beside them, and a report that states a count by hand is refused.

\subsection{Simulation Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\guide{Two parts. \textbf{Simulation:} the simulator and policy (AlpaSim~\cite{alpasim}, VaVAM~\cite{bartoccioni2025vavam}, linear MPC tracking, replayed traffic; planning at 2 Hz and 10 Hz), the knobs, the test scenarios (studies mapped to Euro NCAP tests~\cite{euroncap2023vru,euroncap2023c2c}), repeats and run budgets per method, the metrics (confirmed hardest cases, runs to reach the goal, model time) and the statistical tests. \textbf{Computing environment:} one workstation (GPU model and memory, CPU, RAM, disk, operating system) running two simulations at once on its single GPU; the simulator in containers, with the added retiming and seeding hooks off by default; the agent's model and how it is called (one fresh headless call per decision, read-only tools, time per call); run time (simulated seconds and wall-clock per run at each planning rate, total GPU hours). \emph{Confirm the exact hardware and software versions for the whole campaign; a short table is a common way to report them.}}

\textit{Simulation.} Runs use AlpaSim~\cite{alpasim}, which replays recorded drives as neural reconstructions and closes the loop with the policy under test, VaVAM~\cite{bartoccioni2025vavam}, using the 139k-step checkpoint AlpaSim provides. VaVAM plans from the last eight front-camera frames, 0.5\,s apart. A linear MPC tracks its plan, and the other road users replay their recorded tracks. The ego replays its own recording for the first 4.5\,s, then the policy takes over. Planning runs at VaVAM's own 2\,Hz rhythm or at 10\,Hz with the camera at 2\,Hz. The knobs are a recorded actor's time shift ($-2$ to $+2$\,s) and speed scale ($0.5\times$ to $2\times$), applied by a retiming hook added to AlpaSim's runtime; the ego's speed at the hand-off ($0.6\times$ to $1.4\times$ its recorded speed); and a sideways offset of the plan ($-0.5$ to $+0.5$\,m).

\textit{Studies.} Two main studies were run after the agent's instructions were frozen. In \emph{car cut-in and braking}, a car cuts in or brakes ahead of the ego on 10 scenes that pass as recorded; three knobs give 2,700 settings, the budget is 60 runs at 2\,Hz, and failures are rare. In \emph{lane keeping}, the plan is offset sideways on 12 straight scenes while the ego's speed varies; the budget is 50 runs at 10\,Hz. Three earlier studies at 10\,Hz (a pedestrian crossing, a car cutting in, a car ahead braking) map to the Euro NCAP AEB VRU and car-to-car tests~\cite{euroncap2023vru,euroncap2023c2c}. Each method runs each main study from scratch \verify{7} times and each earlier study 3 times.

\textit{Metrics and statistics.} Per attempt: hardest cases confirmed at the budget, runs to reach the goal (the budget if never), and model time. Methods are compared within a study with an exact two-sided permutation test on the per-attempt values. The planned comparisons are the agent against random search, Optuna and the rules on the two main studies, Holm-corrected over those three; every other comparison is descriptive.

\textit{Computing environment.} Everything ran on one workstation (Table~\ref{tab:env}), two simulations at once on its single GPU. The simulator runs in containers, with the retiming and seeding hooks off by default. The agent is Claude Opus~5.5, called once per decision through the Claude Code command line with no memory between calls. A 2\,Hz rollout takes about 47\,s and a 10\,Hz rollout about 84\,s of wall-clock time, about 3\,min per run with launch and checks; the campaign used \verify{N} GPU hours.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
\guide{\textbf{Paragraph 1: summary.} Restate the two main claims in a few sentences: an agent can run stress testing of a driving policy end to end, unattended, when each action is a checked choice from a fixed menu; and letting it choose the next tests pays off most where failures are rare. Say where the code and data are released.}

\guide{\textbf{Paragraph 2: limitations and future work.} Pair each limitation with the direction that addresses it: one policy under test (test more end-to-end policies); replayed traffic does not react (use reactive traffic); few scenes for some maneuvers (add more scenario types); few repeats per method (run more repeats, and try other models as the agent). Also state the limitations with no direction yet: rendering is less certain far from the recording, and the human check of the scoring is still pending. End with the confirmed hardest cases reused as regression tests.}

SimGate was tested on one policy, VaVAM; testing more end-to-end policies is the next step. Replayed traffic does not react to the ego, so some contacts are replayed cars driving into it; reactive traffic would remove them. The agent varies knobs on recorded scenes, as Euro NCAP-style scenario testing does, and cannot change weather, lighting or road geometry; paired with a simulator that can generate such conditions, it could propose new scenario types and run a study on each. Some maneuvers have few scenes (unprotected left turns: 2 of 250). Each method ran \verify{7} attempts per main study; more attempts, and other models as the agent, would test how far the result carries. Two limitations have no direction yet: rendering is less certain far from the recorded path, where some failures happen (they are flagged), and the human check of the scoring and triage is \verify{pending}. Finally, every confirmed hardest case is exported as an OpenSCENARIO~1.4 concrete scenario, so it can be reused as a regression test.
